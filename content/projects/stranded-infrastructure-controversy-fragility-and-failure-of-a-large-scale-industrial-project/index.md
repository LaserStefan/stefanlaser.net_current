The project examines stranded infrastructure using the failed Intel investment in Magdeburg as a case study. The U.S.-China trade war, chip shortages, and the AI-driven transformation of the semiconductor industry set the stage for Intel’s 2023 announcement that it would build a semiconductor plant in Magdeburg. The venture was seen as a transformative project for Germany and, in particular, Saxony- Anhalt: with ten billion euros in subsidies, it would have been the largest single subsidy for a company in German history. In 2025, however, Intel withdrew the investment. What remained was a halfbuilt industrial production infrastructure, unfulfilled promises, and a site that had been reorganized for Intel: Agricultural land was converted, people were resettled, and transportation, energy, and educational infrastructure was expanded. Now a successor is being sought. Such constellations, in which the realization of production infrastructure is interrupted, have been neglected in sociology to date. The proposal is to treat the planned production infrastructure as an asset: as a financial investment that should secure long-term returns. “Stranding” refers to the collapse of this logic, even though the constructed infrastructure has not disappeared. The project ties into debates on “stranded assets,” in which stranding is, however, predominantly conceived as an endpoint—in contrast, the project aims to grasp stranded infrastructure as a socio-materially entangled and dynamic process, thereby developing an innovative analytical framework. The “After Intel” stands in the tension between a “no longer” and a “not yet.” Three objectives structure the project. First, the public controversy is mapped: the study examines which actors and interests are assembled around this highly contemporary case, how state and corporate actors have shaped the process, and how industrial policy has been translated into regional concerns. To this end, documents from politics, administration, the media, and civil society are analyzed, and interviews are conducted with involved actors. Second, the production infrastructure and its ecologies— supply chains and the natural environment—are explored ethnographically. Walking interviews and excursions to Wroclaw and Dresden reveal how Intel’s establishment continues to have an impact. Third, judgments of failure are examined; precisely because they are contested and contingent, they open up an approach to the question of where the “assetization” specifically failed and what alternatives beyond Intel are virulent. The project contributes to social studies of infrastructure and economic sociology. A mix of qualitative methods guides the research; collaboration partners are integrated through special events. The results will be published in three academic articles, incorporated into a habilitation, and opened up for public discussion.
