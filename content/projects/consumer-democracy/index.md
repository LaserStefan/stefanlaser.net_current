The aim of this research was to link practices and relationships of consumption with issues of democratic control. In this context, I read consumption explicitly not as an individual but as a collective problem. Drawing on approaches from actor-network theory, the focus is placed on the how to assemble collectives of human and non-humans attached to consumption in a democratic fashion.

One research project that emerged from this interest was the “Smart Environment, Smart Information” project. The ‘Internet of Things’ creates environments in which data collection is ubiquitous and thus increasingly difficult to keep track of. This ‘ubiquitous computing’ makes it difficult for users to understand, in some cases, what data is being collected, stored or processed. Furthermore, it may be unclear what consequences arise from the transmission and analysis of this data.

My PhD research, too, was shaped by a collective understanding of consumption, and one case (of this multi-sited ethnography) turned to consumption in a more narrow sense: It discussed a failed smartphone developed by Google called "Ara". It was a modular phone that (so one promise) would have been more customizable and easier to repair. This was a shady promise, no surprise, but it helped understand dynamics of the smartphone industry.
